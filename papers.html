The same question, six angles.

What goes wrong when you bring AI into work that's tightly bound by rules — and what would it take to do it well? Six papers explore this from theory, from data, from four years of building tools inside a regulated workplace, and from the materials it takes to teach the people doing the work.

Paper 1 Under review

The Convergence Lifecycle

When something goes wrong in a regulated organization, who ends up responsible — and was that decided long before the event?

Responsibility doesn't appear at the moment of failure. It's already being shaped — in role definitions, in procedural documents, in the way investigations are designed to look for some causes and not others, and now in how AI systems close off questions before humans have asked them. This paper traces that production across four stages and argues that accountability in regulated work is less a verdict than a slow accumulation. The argument draws on procedural corpus analysis, FDA Warning Letters, and two behavioral studies of how people reason under closure pressure.

Paper 2 Under review

Designed Attribution

Why does aviation think most crashes are human error? Because the investigations are designed to find that answer.

Forty years of NTSB aviation investigations — 71,114 findings and 23,742 cause statements — read two ways. The first reading looks at how investigators code findings: organizational causes appear in 0.58% of records, while individual causes appear nearly six times as often. The second reading looks at the grammar of probable-cause statements: individuals show up as the subject of 74% of sentences, organizations in 0.42%. Both methods converge on the same finding — what aviation calls "human error" is partly a product of how the inquiry is shaped, not just of what happened.

Paper 3 Under review

Designing AI Augmentation Tools for Regulated Work

What I learned building twelve AI tools inside a workplace where you can't afford to break anything.

A reflection from four years inside a GMP-regulated pharmaceutical quality control team — the kind of site HCI rarely gets to study, because outsiders aren't let in and what they'd see can't leave. Across twelve deployed tools, certain design rules kept emerging: don't cross the line into validated systems; let AI draft, let humans decide; treat tacit know-how that senior staff carry as something the tool should make explicit for newer staff. The paper is less about any one tool and more about what designing AI looks like when regulation, judgment, and the people doing the work all have to stay intact.

Paper 4 Under review

Toward Regulatory HCI

HCI knows how to study users. It doesn't yet know how to study users working under rules that govern what AI is even allowed to touch.

Pharmaceutical manufacturing, aviation, clinical care, finance — these are workplaces where AI can't just be deployed; it has to be deployed under rules that decide what it's permitted to do and where human judgment must remain. A position paper proposing that the human–AI interactions in these environments are coherent enough to form a research area in their own right, with shared questions, shared methods, and a comparative framework across regulated industries. The other five papers serve as evidence that the area is already in motion.

Paper 5 Under review

GMP AI Literacy

How do you teach AI to workers whose mistakes would be illegal — and whose judgment regulators trust more than the AI?

A 15-page bilingual Korean–English cookbook and a pixel-art onboarding game called DAY ONE: GMP, both built for frontline QC analysts who needed to use AI without giving up the judgment that makes their work regulated in the first place. The paper walks through the design rationale: why generic AI literacy material doesn't transfer to GMP, what changes when the learner already carries the institutional rules in their head, and how a game can teach things a cookbook can't.

Paper 6 In progress

Augmentation vs Automation in Regulatory Decision-Making

Same task, two AI designs: one helps humans think, one tries to replace them. Which actually works inside a regulated process?

Ten root-cause analysis scenarios from regulated manufacturing, each run through two different AI designs — a human-in-the-loop scaffolding tool I built myself, and a Claude-based agentic pipeline that runs the same task end-to-end. The paper compares not just output quality but where human judgment gets preserved, where it gets quietly elided, and the failure modes that show up only when no one is left in the loop. A direct test of the augmentation-over-automation stance that runs through the rest of the work.

— Research corpora & data infrastructure

What the papers are built on

The empirical foundation: regulatory corpora constructed from public data, plus a four-year field observation archive from inside a GMP-regulated pharmaceutical site.

FDA Warning Letters Corpus
N = 50 letters · 160 violations · 2016–2025

FDA's formal regulatory enforcement documents — issued when significant compliance failures are identified. The pharmaceutical regulator's most consequential public output. Used across the organizational theory, STS, and computational papers.

NTSB Aviation Accident Corpus
71,114 findings · 27,877 narratives · 1982–2025

Full Aviation Accident Database (avall.mdb, ~546 MB) analyzed directly. Methodological contrast with FDA: aviation regulation makes the entire investigative pipeline traceable, whereas FDA makes only final outputs public. The cross-domain comparison provides the strongest external validation site for the lifecycle theory.

Cognitive Friction Corpus
400 sentences · sentence-level annotation

400 naturally occurring sentences from regulated procedural documents, manually annotated at sentence level for cognitive friction features. Empirical base for the CF paper.

Field Observation Archive
~4 years · embedded ethnography

Field notes from inside a GMP-regulated pharmaceutical site (September 2022 – present). The ethnographic ground that the theoretical papers rest on, and the empirical substrate for the design research on the Tools page.

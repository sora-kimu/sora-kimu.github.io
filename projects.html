Tools for navigating chronic illness

What happens when patients have AI on their side — not to diagnose, but to track, translate, match, and name. Each tool tries to lower the cost of one specific kind of unpaid labor that chronic-illness patients quietly do every day.

Patient-facing
🏷

Emotion Namer

If you can't name a feeling, it has nowhere to go. Built on Lieberman's "name it to tame it" research and the Geneva Emotion Wheel (Scherer, 2005): you input a vague word, the tool offers six more precise candidates, and the final pick lands on Russell's valence × arousal plane (1980). Available in Korean, English, and Japanese. If the input is too heavy, the tool stops and surfaces help resources first — not a diagnostic, just a thoughtful friend.

React + React Native Tailwind i18n (KO/EN/JP) LLM API · serverless Geneva Wheel · Russell 2D

Medication Traceability for Crohn's

A daily tracker — currently in active development — that helps people with chronic illness link their medication, symptoms, and side effects over time. The kind of pattern your doctor can't see in a 15-minute appointment. Built for Crohn's specifically rather than chronic disease in general, because doing the medication and symptom data well requires deep familiarity with one condition rather than shallow coverage of many. Designing for daily mobile use, with health data kept local on-device — exportable as a clean PDF for clinic visits.

React Native (mobile) Next.js (web) SQLite + IndexedDB Recharts PDF export

Medical Record Reader

Patients receive clinical reports they can't parse — abbreviations, reference ranges, and discipline-specific jargon that doctors never have time to walk through. This tool reads the report and produces a plain-language version alongside the original, flagging which findings might be worth asking about. It explains; it doesn't diagnose.

Next.js + React Native pdf.js + Tesseract OCR LLM API Tailwind

Clinical Trial Matcher

Trial-finding sites exist, but parsing inclusion/exclusion criteria requires reading dozens of trials carefully — work most patients can't realistically do. This tool takes a patient profile and runs it against the trials' criteria, producing a match percentage with the reason for ineligibility surfaced when the profile doesn't fit.

Terms-of-Service NLP

Most ToS documents are long, dense, and structurally designed to be unread. This project extracts unfair clauses — forced arbitration, unilateral change, broad data-sharing — and tags each with a severity level, producing a quick readable summary of what the document is asking you to agree to. A practice corpus for clause classification, structured on the question of what the document hides.

What I'm working on right now

Not a side project — a study in progress, directly inside the thesis arc: how my own QC colleagues actually use AI, where they trust it, where they don't, and what they're carefully watching out for in a regulated environment.

Ongoing study · in progress
🧭

AI Usage Taxonomy — peer interviews

A study with my QC colleagues — the people who actually work alongside AI tools every day in a regulated environment. The interview asks where they use AI, where they get stuck, where their trust forms, where their distrust forms, and what they're specifically watching out for because the work is GMP-regulated. The output will be a taxonomy of practitioner-AI relationships grounded in the language people actually use to describe their own work — not researcher-imposed categories. Sits inside the broader Regulatory HCI thesis. The taxonomy itself is a contribution; the patterns of where trust forms or breaks are what the dissertation will build on.

Root-Cause Reasoning Scaffolder

A scaffold for investigation reasoning that detects when an explanation is converging too early — and reopens the alternatives still consistent with the evidence. AI as comparison enforcer, not explanation generator.

The problem

Investigations close too early — even when the investigators know better.

When something goes wrong at a pharmaceutical manufacturing site — a deviation, a failed test, a process drift — the company is required to run a root-cause analysis (RCA): a formal investigation that documents what happened, why, and what will be done about it. In principle, an RCA compares multiple plausible explanations before reaching a documented conclusion. In practice, it unfolds under closure pressure — deadlines, documentation requirements, and procedural expectations about when an investigation must be considered complete.

Under these conditions, reasoning doesn't fail to generate alternatives. It fails to maintain them in a comparable state long enough for systematic evaluation. Convergence on a single explanation happens not because the others have been ruled out, but because the comparison between them has collapsed.

A baseline study with twenty experienced practitioners made this visible. At the very first timepoint, 15 of 20 produced single-cause, person-centered explanations. Their own confidence in those explanations averaged 1 out of 5. That number is the whole story: early convergence isn't certainty. It's institutional pressure. Within ten minutes, 18 of 20 had broadened their accounts on their own — they already knew what they were missing.

"In a real investigation, you pick the explanation that closes the file. A systemic cause just opens more questions." — Participant, baseline study
The approach

Not a cognitive problem. A workflow problem.

Most existing work treats premature convergence as a problem of investigator cognition — bias, time pressure, expertise gaps. I reframed it as an interaction problem: the workflow itself doesn't sustain alternatives in a comparable state long enough for meaningful evaluation. Interface design decides which explanations stay visible and which get suppressed before comparison is complete.

I named this failure mode Premature Accountability Convergence (PAC): the early stabilization of causal accounts that occurs when the comparison between alternatives collapses before reasoning has fully developed. PAC is not a cognitive bias. It is a structural outcome — a property of how investigative workflows organize, interrupt, and foreclose comparison under institutional pressure.

The design question shifts

How do we help investigators think better? How do we design workflows that keep comparison open longer?

That reframing led to two design principles. First, comparison preservation: maintain the availability of alternative explanations as an interaction condition, not as a cognitive aid. Second — and this is the one I keep returning to — AI as comparison enforcer: an inversion of the dominant model of AI in investigation support.

A note on what this changed for me

Most AI tools for investigations are built to produce candidate explanations — to narrow the space faster. Designing this scaffold made me realize how often that framing is wrong, especially in regulated work. The investigators in my study already had alternative explanations. What they didn't have was a workflow that let them hold those alternatives open against the pressure to document a conclusion.

What I needed AI to do was the opposite of generation. I needed it to notice when reasoning was narrowing too early and to push back — not by offering its own answer, but by requiring the investigator to revisit theirs. That principle (AI as something that preserves the space for human judgment, not something that fills it) became the through-line for how I think about AI in regulated environments. It's also the principle that shapes the AI literacy materials I write for my team: AI is most trustworthy in this domain when it intervenes on the conditions of reasoning, not on the conclusions.

What I built

A five-stage scaffold, with AI in one specific role.

I designed and deployed a five-stage scaffold that restructures the conditions of investigation reasoning. The AI component is integrated into Stage 3 only, and only as a conditional trigger that reopens the comparison space when it detects convergence signals. It does not suggest causes, evaluate hypotheses, or generate conclusions.

  1. 1
    Intake
    Capture the deviation event and the investigator's first hypotheses — without committing to one.
  2. 2
    Hypothesis expansion
    Surface alternative explanations across causal categories — person, procedure, system, equipment, environment — and make each one visible in parallel.
  3. 3
    Evidence weighing AI
    As the investigator maps evidence to hypotheses, the AI watches for convergence signals — narrowing, dropping, or implicit dismissal of alternatives. When it detects one, it prompts a return to the suppressed hypothesis. The AI never produces an answer; it just refuses to let comparison silently collapse.
  4. 4
    Comparison check
    A structured comparison view: each remaining hypothesis side by side, with its evidence and what would be needed to rule it out.
  5. 5
    Pre-closure verification
    Before final attribution can be documented, a required check confirms alternatives were considered — and surfaces what was chosen not to pursue, so the audit trail reflects the reasoning actually performed.
Who this is for

An intervention at one specific moment.

This tool isn't designed for a general user category. It's designed for a specific moment: the point between evidence collection and documentation, when comparison currently collapses. Investigators reach this moment under closure pressure, with multiple plausible causes still on the table but no workflow support for holding them open. The scaffold meets them there — not earlier, not later.

More precisely
Practitioners conducting deviation investigations in regulated manufacturing, at the moment they transition from understanding what happened to documenting why. Reviewers, managers, and approvers benefit indirectly — the scaffold produces a record that reflects what was actually considered, including what was chosen not to pursue. But the primary intervention is for the person doing the reasoning, at the moment they're most likely to foreclose it.
Results

Broader reasoning, no loss of confidence, unanimous adoption intent.

Twenty experienced practitioners used the scaffold on real deviation cases:

18/ 20
produced broader causal accounts with the scaffold than they did at baseline.
12/ 15
reconsidered their initial attribution and shifted from person-centered to systemic explanations.
5.0/ 5
average usability rating, with 4.7 for PAC mitigation and 4.9 for perceived usefulness.
20/ 20
expressed adoption intent — while simultaneously naming the audit trail as a structural barrier to deployment.

Confidence ratings were maintained or slightly decreased while causal breadth increased substantially. That dissociation matters: in high-accountability environments, expanded reasoning without confidence collapse is exactly what comparison-preserving design needs to produce. Investigators reasoned more openly without becoming less sure of their conclusions.

"The problem isn't the tool. The problem is that the tool would show that we looked at the systemic cause and decided not to pursue it. That's a conversation no one wants to have." — Participant, on the organizational adoption problem
Takeaway

Comparison collapse is addressable through design — and the next problem is the audit trail.

Three things this work shows:

01
Premature convergence is structural, not cognitive. Confidence ratings of 1 out of 5 at the moment of early convergence say it plainly: investigators already know their first explanation is incomplete. The workflow forecloses comparison before they can act on what they know.
02
AI as comparison enforcer is a workable inversion. In regulated environments, an AI that produces conclusions creates compliance risk. An AI that detects when comparison has collapsed and triggers its reopening preserves human judgment while addressing the structural problem. This is the model I keep returning to in everything I design here.
03
The hardest design problem isn't the tool. Comparison-preserving scaffolding makes visible a choice that organizations have been able to leave invisible — the choice not to pursue a systemic cause. Whether organizations can tolerate that legibility is a regulatory and organizational question, not a design one. But it's the question this work is now positioned to ask.

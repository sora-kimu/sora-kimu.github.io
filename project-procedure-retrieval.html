Layer 1 · Accessibility · Design prototype

Procedure Retrieval

A semantic search layer over a regulated archive — not just to find procedures faster, but to expand the range of procedures a practitioner considers reachable, comparable, and usable in the first place.

The problem

When you can't reach the procedure, the procedure doesn't exist.

Standard operating procedures in pharmaceutical manufacturing are legally binding documents. They define how processes must be performed, what counts as acceptable deviation, and what actions can be defended after the fact. They're among the most consequential documents in regulated work.

Their practical force, though, depends on something the regulations don't address: whether they're reachable at the moment action is required. Archives sit as scanned PDFs that block keyword search. Titles are too similar to distinguish. Time pressure makes trial-and-error retrieval expensive. Faced with these conditions, practitioners don't simply fail to comply. They work from memory, defer action, or ask a colleague.

Prior research has documented this substitution behavior — one study found that 18.4% of clinical trial staff sought a colleague's guidance rather than retrieving the relevant procedure. But the diagnosis usually stops at "improve the search." This work asked something different: what does retrieval design actually change about how practitioners interpret and act?

"Dying of busyness while procedure search is also work." — Participant, describing daily retrieval burden
"When you spend time just finding the right procedure, you are already tired before you even start reading it." — Participant, on the downstream effect on judgment
The approach

Retrieval isn't downstream of work. It shapes what work becomes thinkable.

The dominant framing of procedure retrieval is utilitarian: faster search = less wasted time. I reframed it as something more consequential — an upstream condition on interpretation. Before a practitioner reads a procedure, compares it to another, or decides whether to act on it, they have already spent effort navigating whether the relevant procedure is even reachable. That navigation isn't preparation for the work. It's part of the work — and it shapes which procedures the practitioner ever encounters at all.

The design question shifts

How do we help practitioners find procedures faster? How does retrieval design shape what practitioners consider available to use?

That reframing came with the same constraint I keep meeting in regulated work: the validated archive cannot be modified. Source documents are part of a formally validated system; restructuring them isn't on the table. So the retrieval layer had to operate externally — reading the archive, indexing it, surfacing it — without touching anything underneath.

A note on what this changed for me

Working on this layer made me see how often retrieval gets discussed as a solved problem. "Just add search" is the standard response. But once I started watching practitioners work, it became clear that retrieval design is interpretation design. The set of procedures a practitioner can actually reach shapes the set of procedures they can compare, justify using, and act on with confidence. The interface isn't downstream support for work — it sets the conditions under which work becomes thinkable.

That realization is what eventually pulled me toward an AI support layer as an extension. Once I saw that retrieval was an interpretive act, the next question became: can AI extend the cues available to practitioners without taking over interpretation? This is the same principle I bring to the AI literacy materials I write for my team — AI as something that expands the interpretive surface, not something that resolves it.

What I built

A retrieval layer — and then an AI support layer extending it.

The work proceeded in two phases. The first was the prototype I evaluated with practitioners. The second was a conceptual extension that addressed a residual problem the prototype made visible: interpretive ambiguity beyond retrieval itself.

Phase 1 · Built & evaluated

Keyword-searchable retrieval layer

An external retrieval layer over a corpus of 500 synthetic procedures modeled on real regulated archives. Practitioners could enter task terms — what they were trying to do — rather than document titles or identifiers they had to remember in advance.

Each result surfaced contextual cues to help narrow the search space before opening any document: document identifiers, category labels, match-strength indicators, and short text snippets. The goal was to make the comparison space visible at the retrieval stage, so that selection decisions could be informed rather than guessed.

Throughout, the layer operated externally — reading the archive, never modifying it. No changes to validated source documents. No automated compliance decisions.

Phase 2 · Conceptual extension

AI support layer for interpretive burden

The retrieval prototype reduced navigational burden. It didn't address what came next: the interpretive burden of deciding which retrieved procedure was the right one, how its scope related to adjacent procedures, and where ambiguity in its language might produce inconsistent application. Participants who successfully located procedures still reported uncertainty.

I designed an AI support layer organized around three explicit principles, made visible in the interface itself:

Explains
why a result appeared. Score breakdown by keyword, title, section, and body match — so practitioners can evaluate whether the basis for retrieval is relevant to their task.
Surfaces
related procedures. Adjacent documents from cross-reference mappings, so the comparison space is available without practitioners having to reconstruct it.
Keeps
alternative interpretations visible. Rule-based notes flag where similar tasks are governed by overlapping procedures — without resolving the ambiguity.

The interface makes the design constraint explicit: "this note keeps alternative interpretations visible. It does not replace reading or make compliance decisions." Empty results are reframed as signal rather than failure — information about archive structure, not evidence of user error.

This layer was a design direction rather than a separately evaluated prototype. It instantiates the judgment-preservation principle that governs every tool in this framework: AI expands the cues available to practitioners without producing outputs that function as quality decisions.

Who this is for

An intervention at the moment before reading begins.

This tool is designed for the moment before — before a practitioner opens a procedure, before they compare it to another, before they decide it applies. That moment currently absorbs hidden labor: archive memorization, naming-convention learning, asking-a-colleague, working-from-memory. The retrieval layer meets practitioners at that moment and changes what's reachable.

More precisely
Practitioners consulting procedures in regulated manufacturing — at the point of action, under time pressure, where the cost of unreachable documents currently falls on memory and informal mediation. Less experienced practitioners benefit most: they're the ones currently bearing the highest cost of archive-naming opacity, because they haven't had years to internalize which terms map to which procedures. Senior practitioners benefit too — the layer externalizes some of the disambiguation labor they currently provide informally to newer staff.
Results

Faster retrieval, willing reuse, and a finding the design didn't predict.

Twenty experienced practitioners worked through realistic procedure-retrieval tasks using the prototype:

6 → 1min
average exploratory retrieval time, reduced from approximately six minutes under current conditions to approximately one minute with the prototype.
18/ 20
rated the system intuitive overall. The two exceptions attributed hesitation to result-discrimination difficulty in the synthetic corpus, not to interface design.
4.5/ 5
average rating for result cues — identifiers, category labels, match indicators, contextual snippets — as useful in narrowing the search space.
20/ 20
expressed willingness to reuse the system in live archive conditions.

These numbers were expected. What wasn't fully expected was what participants described next.

The deeper finding
When access changes, behavior changes

Retrieval design reshapes the behavioral repertoire — not just the search time.

When asked what changed for them under the prototype, participants described a pattern that the design hadn't been built to address. Under current archive conditions, the practical difficulty of locating unfamiliar procedures leads practitioners to operate within a personally known subset of the archive — consulting procedures they've used before rather than searching for procedures that might be more directly applicable.

The procedures they don't reach aren't simply harder to find. They're operationally invisible: not part of the set of actions a practitioner considers available, comparable, or defensible. When the cost of retrieval is high, the archive shrinks — silently, individually, to whatever each practitioner can carry in memory.

The prototype made a wider procedural landscape accessible, and participants reported that this changed more than navigational ease. It changed what they felt able to consider, compare, and defend using. Accessibility interventions don't just accelerate pre-existing workflow. They reshape the behavioral repertoire within which interpretation occurs.

And the cost of inaccessible documents isn't distributed evenly. Senior practitioners described informal disambiguation labor they routinely provide to newer staff — explaining which procedure name maps to which equipment, why two similarly-named procedures aren't interchangeable. That labor is invisible to the quality system, but it's real, recurring, and concentrated on whoever has been around long enough to know. Making retrieval easier moves some of that labor from senior practitioners into the design layer — where it can be addressed structurally rather than carried personally.

Takeaway

Accessibility is a design condition on interpretation — and a fairness condition on practice.

Three things this work shows:

01
Retrieval design shapes interpretation, not just efficiency. The set of procedures a practitioner can reach defines the set of procedures they can compare and act on. Reducing retrieval burden doesn't just save time — it expands the procedural landscape within which judgment occurs. That's a design effect on what work becomes thinkable, not just on how long it takes.
02
Judgment-preserving AI is a coherent design direction, not just a constraint. The AI support layer organized around explains, surfaces, and keeps shows what AI can do in regulated work without crossing into compliance decisions: extend the interpretive surface without resolving it. The constraint isn't a limitation on what AI can do here — it's the design specification.
03
Accessibility redistributes labor. The cost of inaccessible documentation currently falls on memory, informal mediation, and the disambiguation labor senior practitioners absorb. Making the archive structurally legible — externalizing the naming conventions, the cross-references, the interpretive cues — moves that cost out of individuals and into the system. This is the equity dimension of design in regulated work, and it sits at the foundation of every other layer.

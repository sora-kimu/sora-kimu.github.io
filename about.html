Hi, I'm Sora! 😊🐚👋
(소라 means "conch shell" in Korean — yes, the 🐚 is literally my name.)

I'm a practitioner-researcher in pharmaceutical GMP quality control in South Korea. My research program studies accountability infrastructure — how responsibility gets produced in regulated work, and what happens to that production when AI enters the workflow. The methods draw on organizational theory, STS, HCI, and computational text analysis, but the core question is organizational: how do accountability structures get built, reshaped, and sometimes quietly closed off?

AI integration into regulated work isn't a question of whether anymore — it's happening. What I actually spend time on is the how: how organizations adopt AI without losing the accountability structures that make regulated work legible in the first place. Most of my days are spent between studying those dynamics and building tools that try to take them seriously.

The short version: I spend a lot of time thinking about why organizational outcomes feel inevitable when they're actually designed that way — and what happens when AI enters that design. It's a great conversation starter. At parties. That I go to. Occasionally.

How I got here (and why I want a PhD) 🙃

The research grew on the side of a full-time QC role — no lab, no advisor, no institutional scaffolding. It didn't start as a research program either. It started with noticing things at work that seemed important and couldn't find in the literature the way I wanted to read about them, so eventually I started writing myself. Every time a new question came up, I'd go figure out what method might answer it — googling, reading, learning the technique from scratch, trying it, getting it wrong, trying again. That's how the whole thing grew: question first, then method, then paper. Not the usual direction.

(I once spent three weeks going in circles on a theoretical framework with no one to ask. It was fine. I'm fine. We don't talk about it.)

What it turned into: a four-stage accountability lifecycle theory, nine production tools deployed at a GMP-regulated site (with N=20 user studies on five of them), a computational analysis of FDA Warning Letters, and 14 papers — four currently under review at organization theory journals, the rest working papers spanning design, computational methods, and theoretical synthesis. I also designed and continue to iterate on an AI integration training program for my QC team, with a differentiated curriculum for managers (building AI-assisted tools) and operators (AI-assisted document work). Teaching people to use AI inside a regulated workflow has sharpened the questions I want to pursue next — especially how organizations adopt AI without eroding the accountability structures that make regulated work legible in the first place.

Which is exactly why I want a PhD. Working this way taught me a lot, but it also taught me what I'm missing. When you learn methods by finding them yourself, one question at a time, you get fluent at the ones you've needed — and completely blind to the ones you haven't. I want proper methodological training from people who've been doing this for decades, not another self-taught workaround that gets me 80% of the way there. I've also written 14 papers without anyone to tell me when a draft is actually done, which means I've spent a lot of time guessing at the ceiling of "good enough," and occasionally re-inventing things that already had names. But honestly, the hardest part hasn't been any of that. It's been the absence of intellectual community — no one to argue with, no one to push back on an idea at 11pm, no one who cares about the same strange questions you do. It's a kind of loneliness that's hard to describe until you've spent a few years inside it, and it's the part of a PhD I'm most looking forward to. Maybe more than anything else.

The tools and empirical work are my contribution. The theoretical depth comes from training I haven't had. In doctoral work, I expect to collaborate — with CS researchers on algorithmic components, organizational scholars on fieldwork methods, and regulatory experts on policy interfaces. My strength is in deployment and synthesis; the parts I can't yet do alone are exactly what doctoral training exists for.

Applying Fall 2027. 🎯

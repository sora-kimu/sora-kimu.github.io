Review Friction Visibility

A dashboard that surfaces hidden coordination work in document review — making friction visible before it becomes a missed deadline, without modifying the validated systems underneath.

The problem

Review friction accumulates — but no one can see it accumulating.

Deviation reports and corrective-action documents in pharmaceutical manufacturing pass through multiple reviewers — quality assurance, quality control, subject-matter experts, department managers — across iterative rounds of comment, revision, and approval. These review processes are routinely experienced as slow. But the usual diagnosis — too many reviewers, unclear ownership, insufficient training — misidentifies the problem.

The real issue isn't the delay itself. It's that the coordination work producing the delay is structurally invisible. A wording comment triggers a revision cycle. A reviewer reopens a document closed in a previous round. A pattern of minor but recurring feedback generates disproportionate turnaround time across a document class. These patterns are real, consequential, and structural — but they remain distributed across individual comment threads, document versions, and reviewer roles. No single person has a complete view of them.

Friction becomes visible only after delays have already escalated, and accountability is assigned retroactively — without access to the coordination conditions that produced the outcome.

The approach

Not an efficiency problem. An observability problem.

The standard response to slow review is to optimize coordination — fewer reviewers, faster handoffs, automated routing. I reframed it as an interaction problem: the workflow doesn't make coordination work visible at the moment it could be interpreted and acted upon. The design question isn't how do we make review faster. It's how do we make friction legible while it's still upstream of failure.

The design question shifts

How do we optimize the review process? How do we make hidden coordination work legible before failure?

That reframing introduced a design boundary that became central to everything that followed: judgment preservation. In regulated manufacturing, quality decisions must be made and documented by qualified human reviewers. Any system that automates or pre-empts those decisions doesn't just feel intrusive — it creates a compliance risk. The design challenge was therefore to make friction visible while leaving judgment intact.

A note on what this changed for me

Most workflow tools want to recommend, decide, route. Designing this dashboard taught me that in regulated environments, the more useful intervention is often the opposite: surface, don't decide. The dashboard never tells a reviewer what to do — it shows them what's already happening, in a form their existing judgment can act on.

That principle — AI and computational tools as observability layers rather than decision-makers — became one of my core design commitments. It's the same principle I bring to the AI literacy materials I write for my team: the most trustworthy AI in this domain is often the AI that helps you see more clearly, not the AI that tells you what to think.

What I built

Two components, neither one a decision-maker.

I designed and deployed two components, both operating as external observability layers on top of validated documentation systems. Neither modifies what regulations require. Neither automates quality decisions.

  1. 1
    Six-tab workflow dashboard

    Surfaces review burden, comment distribution, and document-journey patterns as interpretable signals. Each tab is a different lens on the same review record: Heatmap (turnaround by reviewer role and document type), Checklist (pre-review scaffold), Document Journey (round-by-round timeline of a single document), Before/After (anticipated effects across five workflow dimensions), Reviewer Simulation (historical category-level patterns), and a top-level summary view.

    Built on a synthetic dataset of 100 deviation-review records. The whole structure lets reviewers compare across documents and roles without any individual reviewer having to manually compile the picture.

  2. 2
    TF-IDF pattern detection layer

    Identifies recurring issue clusters across review cycles. Groups similar comments across documents and reviewers without manual tagging — surfacing the structural categories of friction accumulation that individual comment threads can't reveal on their own.

    The layer produces pattern summaries, not recommendations: it describes what has happened, not what should happen next. A reviewer whose wording comments are consistently associated with long turnaround times can finally see that pattern — without the system deciding what to do about it.

Who this is for

An intervention at one specific moment.

This tool isn't designed for a general user category. It's designed for the moment between submission and closure — when a document is moving through review rounds and friction is accumulating, but no one has the picture yet. The dashboard meets the workflow there, not earlier and not after the failure.

More precisely
Quality assurance and quality control reviewers handling deviation and corrective-action documents in regulated manufacturing. Submitters use it to see where their document stands and what kind of review activity is generating delay. Reviewers use it to monitor and regulate their own workload across documents. The same dashboard speaks to both sides of the coordination — a shared infrastructure for a problem currently distributed across both.
Results

High ratings, full adoption intent, and one dissociation that matters.

Twenty experienced practitioners evaluated both components:

5.0/ 5
average usability rating on both the dashboard and the pattern detection layer.
20/ 20
expressed adoption intent, with role-differentiated framing emerging spontaneously.
100%
anticipated reduced accountability asymmetry — across both submitter and reviewer roles, without prompting.
2
distinct legibility profiles: the checklist was immediately accessible; the pattern detection layer required initial conceptual orientation. Both arrived at equivalent ratings.

That last result is the study's central empirical finding — and it took some interpretation. The checklist was immediately legible because it addresses a problem practitioners already recognize: inconsistent review preparation. The pattern detection layer required initial orientation because it addresses a problem practitioners haven't previously had the tools to name — structural friction accumulation. The unfamiliarity didn't produce rejection; once framed, all twenty participants endorsed it as useful.

"I never thought of revision loops as something you could measure. But once you see it that way, it's obvious that some documents are just structurally harder." — Participant, on the pattern detection layer

Usefulness and immediate legibility aren't the same thing. A tool can be highly useful for a problem practitioners haven't yet learned to see. That's not a usability failure — it's a sign that building the problem category is itself part of the design work.

An unexpected finding
What participants said when I wasn't asking

Friction visibility became fairness infrastructure.

The most consequential response from participants wasn't about usability. It was about what visibility of review burden would change organizationally. All twenty anticipated reduced accountability asymmetry from the dashboard — and this emerged consistently across both submitter and reviewer roles, without me prompting for it.

"The delay gets attributed to whoever submitted the document, not to the review structure that produced it." — Participant
"This would make it harder to blame the person at the end of the queue when the problem was in the middle of the process." — Participant

When coordination patterns weren't visible, participants described delay in outcome terms — who was at the end of the chain, who closed the file. When friction patterns were surfaced, delay was reinterpreted as emerging from structural accumulation across the whole workflow. Visibility didn't eliminate responsibility. It redistributed it — tying responsibility to observable coordination conditions instead of inferred outcomes.

I designed the dashboard to make friction legible. Participants told me it would make accountability fairer. That's a finding the design didn't predict — and it reframes what observability is for in regulated work. Not just workflow support. Fairness infrastructure.

Takeaway

Observability isn't a secondary feature. It's part of the interaction.

Three things this work shows:

01
Review friction is an observability problem, not an efficiency problem. The coordination work generating delay is real and structural — but no single participant has a complete view of it. The intervention isn't to speed up the workflow. It's to make the workflow's structure visible at the moment that visibility can change something.
02
Judgment-preserving design is a compliance principle, not just a normative one. In regulated environments, computational outputs must support human interpretive authority, not substitute for it. That constraint isn't a limitation — it's a design direction that produces a specific kind of tool: one that surfaces, never decides.
03
Visibility changes how accountability is distributed. Making coordination conditions legible doesn't reduce accountability — it redistributes it from the person at the end of the chain to the structural conditions that produced the outcome. That's the unexpected finding the design didn't predict, and it points to the next research question: whether organizations can adopt tools that produce that kind of legibility.

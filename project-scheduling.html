Operational tool · Deployed

Project Scheduling Tool

A morning briefing tool for QC project managers — who don't run tests, only chase them. Each day it tells the PM who to check on and what to ask, with pre-written messages that lower the emotional cost of asking.

The problem

Three PMs chase information they don't own — and asking has an emotional tax.

The Customer Centric team coordinates QC testing across stability, batch release, and tech transfer projects. The team is three people. None of them run tests — they manage timelines, chase operators, and report to clients.

Test progress lives in the operators' heads. Asking has both time cost and emotional cost — operators are already busy, and repeatedly chasing them feels like nagging. So PMs often don't ask. So deadlines slip. So they only find out when it's too late, and they're the ones who have to explain why a deadline was missed when they never had the information to prevent it.

On the surface this looks like a scheduling failure. But the structure underneath is information asymmetry with an emotional tax: PMs are receivers of information that lives with senders who have no incentive to send. The tool isn't trying to make the PMs schedule better. It's trying to lower the cost of asking until asking becomes routine.

What it does

A daily briefing. The system asks; the PM doesn't have to.

Backward-derived checkpoints
From the deadline (stability +25 working days, batch release +30 calendar days), the seven-stage timeline is auto-laid-out. PMs don't plan — the system proposes where each project should be today.
Pre-written Teams messages
"Copy message" → polite, system-framed phrasing already drafted → paste into Teams. The PM never composes from scratch, never wonders if they sound nagging.
"It's the system asking"
Each prompt frames itself as a scheduled checkpoint, not a personal request. The PM isn't nagging — the system is surfacing a standard-timing check. The emotional cost shifts from the PM to the tool.
Black-hole tracking
Unassigned tests trigger D+2 yellow, D+5 red. Critical during staff transitions when ownership easily lapses — orphaned tasks become visible before they're forgotten.
Per-test granularity
Tech transfer projects with 10+ test methods stay legible — each method tracked independently, so multi-method bottlenecks don't hide inside a single project block.
Operator-centric view
Cross-cuts projects to surface operator capacity and mapping. Knowing who's overloaded matters as much as knowing what's due.
Two paths · one chosen

Don't change the senders. Make the receivers' job lighter.

Once the problem clarified as information asymmetry, two solution shapes were possible: force operators to report, or work around them. The first would require cross-team political buy-in, SOP/KPI changes outside the CC team's control, and a long timeline before any value. The second could ship from inside the team, with zero burden added to operators, and create immediate observable effect.

Design principle

A spec is a starting point, not an endpoint. The original brief proposed kanban boards, AI prioritization, and four tabs — a standard project-management feature list. Stopping to ask what problem this actually solves revealed something different: the tool wasn't for the people doing the work, it was for the people chasing it. The most useful thing was refusing to build the obvious thing long enough to find the actual problem. Stopping is the move.

In daily use

Deployed to the team I work with.

Used by the three QC project managers daily — opened first thing in the morning. The five-minute briefing tells them who to check on, what to ask, and gives them the words. They check, paste, and move on with their day. The hardest design choice was what not to put on this screen.

Deployed

Used daily by the CC PM team. Replaced a paper notebook that depended on each PM's memory. Includes reproduction guide and team training documentation — including the operator-respect framing so future users keep the cost-of-asking principle intact.
